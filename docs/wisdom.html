般若波羅蜜多心經

無苦集滅道，無智亦無得。以無所得故，菩提薩埵，依般若波羅蜜多故，心無罣礙。

There is no suffering, no cause of suffering, no cessation, no path; no wisdom and no attainment. With nothing to attain, a bodhisattva relies on Prajna Paramita, and the mind is without hindrance.

孟子・滕文公上

徐子以告孟子。孟子曰：「夫夷子，信以為人之親其兄之子為若親其鄰之赤子乎？彼有取爾也。赤子匍匐將入井，非赤子之罪也。且天之生物也，使之一本，而夷子二本故也。蓋上世嘗有不葬其親者。其親死，則舉而委之於壑。他日過之，狐狸食之，蠅蚋姑嘬之。其顙有泚，睨而不視。夫泚也，非為人泚，中心達於面目。蓋歸反虆梩而掩之。掩之誠是也，則孝子仁人之掩其親，亦必有道矣。」徐子以告夷子。夷子憮然為閒曰：「命之矣。」

Xu Bi reported this to Mencius. Mencius said, 'Does Yi Zhi truly believe that a person's love for his brother's child is the same as his love for a neighbor's infant? The passage he cited has a specific point: when an infant crawls toward a well, it is not the infant's fault — this is what it means. Moreover, Heaven produces living things from a single root, but Yi Zhi would give them two roots. Presumably in ancient times there were those who did not bury their parents. When their parents died, they picked up the bodies and threw them into a ditch. Later, passing by, they saw foxes and wild cats gnawing at them and flies and gnats swarming over them. Sweat broke out on their foreheads; they looked away and could not bear to look. That sweating was not for the sake of others — it was a feeling from the innermost heart breaking through to the face. They went home, returned with baskets and shovels, and covered the bodies. If covering them was truly right, then filial children and benevolent people who cover their parents must also have a proper Way of doing so.' Xu Bi reported this to Yi Zhi. Yi Zhi stood in a daze for a moment and said, 'He has taught me.'

六祖大師法寶壇經・定慧品第四

念者念真如本性。真如即是念之體，念即是真如之用。真如自性起念，非眼耳鼻舌能念。真如有性，所以起念；真如若無，眼耳色聲當時即壞。善知識！真如自性起念，六根雖有見聞覺知，不染萬境，而真性常自在。故經云：『能善分別諸法相，於第一義而不動。』」。

'"Thought" means thought of Suchness, of one's true original nature. Suchness is the substance of thought; thought is the function of Suchness. It is the Suchness of one's own nature that gives rise to thought — not the eyes, ears, nose, or tongue that can think. Suchness possesses this nature, and therefore thought arises. If Suchness were absent, then eyes, ears, forms, and sounds would instantly disintegrate. Good friends! When the Suchness of one's own nature gives rise to thought, the six sense faculties may see, hear, perceive, and know, yet they are not stained by the myriad objects, and the true nature is always free and autonomous. Therefore the sutra says: "One who can skillfully distinguish all dharma-characteristics remains unmoved in the ultimate, first meaning."'
