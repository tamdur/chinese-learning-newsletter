般若波羅蜜多心經

無罣礙故，無有恐怖，遠離顛倒夢想，究竟涅槃。三世諸佛，依般若波羅蜜多故，得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。

Without hindrance, there is no fear. Far from all inverted views, one attains nirvana. All buddhas of past, present, and future rely on Prajna Paramita and attain supreme, perfect enlightenment.

孟子 · 滕文公上

然友復之鄒問孟子。孟子曰：「然。不可以他求者也。孔子曰：『君薨，聽於冢宰。歠粥，面深墨。即位而哭，百官有司，莫敢不哀，先之也。』上有好者，下必有甚焉者矣。『君子之德，風也；小人之德，草也。草尚之風必偃。』是在世子。」然友反命。世子曰：「然。是誠在我。」五月居廬，未有命戒。百官族人可謂曰知。及至葬，四方來觀之，顏色之戚，哭泣之哀，弔者大悅。

Ran You went again to Zou and asked Mencius. Mencius said, 'Yes. This is something where one cannot look to others. Confucius said: "When a ruler dies, authority is entrusted to the chief minister. The heir drinks thin gruel, and his face turns deeply dark. He takes his place and weeps, and none among the officials and functionaries dares not to grieve, because he has led by example." When those above have a preference, those below will surely carry it to excess. "The virtue of the gentleman is like the wind; the virtue of the common people is like the grass. When the wind blows over the grass, the grass must bend." This rests with the heir apparent.' Ran You returned and reported. The heir apparent said, 'Yes. This truly rests with me.' For five months he dwelt in the mourning hut, issuing no commands or instructions. The officials and kinsmen all acknowledged his wisdom. When the funeral came, people from all directions came to observe. The grief in his expression and the sorrow of his weeping greatly pleased those who came to offer condolences.

六祖大師法寶壇經 · 疑問品第三

武帝心邪，不知正法。造寺度僧、布施設齋，名為求福，不可將福便為功德。功德在法身中，不在修福。」師又曰：「見性是功，平等是德。念念無滯，常見本性，真實妙用，名為功德。內心謙下是功，外行於禮是德。自性建立萬法是功，心體離念是德。不離自性是功，應用無染是德。若覓功德法身，但依此作，是真功德。若修功德之人，心即不輕，常行普敬。心常輕人，吾我不斷，即自無功；自性虛妄不實，即自無德。為吾我自大，常輕一切故。

Emperor Wu's mind was misguided; he did not understand the true Dharma. Building temples, ordaining monks, giving alms, and providing feasts — this is called seeking blessings. Blessings should not be confused with merit. Merit resides in the Dharma-body, not in the cultivation of blessings.' The Master continued: 'Seeing one's own nature is merit; equanimity is virtue. When thought after thought flows without obstruction, and one constantly sees one's true nature in its genuine and wondrous function — this is called merit and virtue. Inward humility of mind is merit; outward practice of propriety is virtue. Establishing all dharmas from one's own nature is merit; the mind-essence being free from thought is virtue. Not departing from one's own nature is merit; responding to situations without defilement is virtue. If you seek the merit and virtue of the Dharma-body, simply act according to this — that is true merit and virtue. Those who cultivate merit and virtue do not look down on others in their minds, but always practice universal respect. If the mind constantly looks down on others and the sense of self never ceases, then one has no merit. If one's own nature is false and unreal, then one has no virtue — because the inflated ego constantly belittles everything else.'
