般若波羅蜜多心經 — Heart Sutra — Sunday

故知般若波羅蜜多，是大神咒，是大明咒，是無上咒，是無等等咒，能除一切苦，真實不虛。故說般若波羅蜜多咒，即說咒曰：揭諦揭諦，波羅揭諦，波羅僧揭諦，菩提薩婆訶。

Therefore, know that Prajna Paramita is the great transcendent mantra, the great bright mantra, the supreme mantra, the unequalled mantra, which can remove all suffering and is true, not false. Therefore he proclaimed the Prajna Paramita mantra, saying: Gate gate paragate parasamgate bodhi svaha.

孟子 — 公孫丑上

孟子曰：「矢人豈不仁於函人哉？矢人唯恐不傷人，函人唯恐傷人。巫匠亦然，故術不可不慎也。孔子曰：『里仁為美。擇不處仁，焉得智？』夫仁，天之尊爵也，人之安宅也。莫之禦而不仁，是不智也。不仁、不智、無禮、無義，人役也。人役而恥為役，由弓人而恥為弓，矢人而恥為矢也。如恥之，莫如為仁。仁者如射，射者正己而後發。發而不中，不怨勝己者，反求諸己而已矣。」

Mencius said: "Is the arrow-maker really less benevolent than the armor-maker? The arrow-maker's only fear is that his arrows will not harm people; the armor-maker's only fear is that people will be harmed. The same is true of the healer and the coffin-maker. Therefore, one must be careful in choosing one's profession. Confucius said: 'It is beautiful to dwell among the benevolent. If one chooses not to dwell among the benevolent, how can one be considered wise?' Benevolence is Heaven's most honored rank and the most secure dwelling of humankind. When nothing prevents one from being benevolent and yet one is not—this is a lack of wisdom. One who is neither benevolent, nor wise, nor observant of propriety, nor righteous—such a person is a slave. To be a slave and feel ashamed of being a slave is like being a bow-maker who is ashamed of making bows, or an arrow-maker who is ashamed of making arrows. If one truly feels the shame, the best course is to practice benevolence. The benevolent person is like an archer: the archer first corrects his own posture, then lets fly. If the arrow misses the mark, he does not resent those who surpassed him but turns inward to examine himself."

六祖大師法寶壇經 — 行由品第一

「次日，祖潛至碓坊，見能腰石舂米，語曰：『求道之人，為法忘軀，當如是乎！』乃問曰：『米熟也未？』惠能曰：『米熟久矣，猶欠篩在。』祖以杖擊碓三下而去。惠能即會祖意，三鼓入室；祖以袈裟遮圍，不令人見，為說《金剛經》。至『應無所住而生其心』，惠能言下大悟，一切萬法，不離自性。遂啟祖言：『何期自性，本自清淨；何期自性，本不生滅；何期自性，本自具足；何期自性，本無動搖；何期自性，能生萬法。

The next day, the Patriarch came secretly to the rice-pounding room and saw Huineng with a stone tied to his waist, pounding rice. He said, 'A seeker of the Way, forgetting his body for the Dharma — should it not be just so!' Then he asked, 'Is the rice ready yet?' Huineng replied, 'The rice has been ready for a long time — it still needs sifting.' The Patriarch tapped the mortar three times with his staff and left. Huineng immediately understood the Patriarch's meaning. At the third watch, he entered the Patriarch's chamber. The Patriarch used his kashaya robe as a screen so that no one could see, and expounded the Diamond Sutra. When he reached the passage 'Give rise to a mind that abides nowhere,' Huineng was instantly and completely awakened: all the myriad dharmas are inseparable from one's own nature. He said to the Patriarch: 'Who would have thought that the self-nature is originally pure! Who would have thought that the self-nature is originally neither born nor destroyed! Who would have thought that the self-nature is originally complete in itself! Who would have thought that the self-nature is originally without agitation! Who would have thought that the self-nature can give rise to all dharmas!'
